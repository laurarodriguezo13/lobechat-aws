## Assumptions

The cost model prices the prod architecture defined in Q2 against the following workload parameters, held constant across all three scales. All prices are AWS list prices for **eu-west-1** as of May 2026.

| Parameter | Value | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Messages per user per day | 10 | Typical knowledge-worker AI assistant usage, comparable to daily Slack message volume |
| Avg input tokens per message | 500 | System prompt ~200 tok + user query ~300 tok |
| Avg output tokens per message | 800 | Typical assistant response with context |
| LLM routing mix | 80% Claude 3 Haiku / 20% Claude 3.5 Sonnet | Haiku for standard Q&A; Sonnet for MCP-heavy agentic tasks requiring multi-step tool orchestration |
| RAG queries per message | 2 | One semantic search + one document-fetch via Qdrant / Hayhooks pipeline |
| Embedding tokens per RAG query | 256 | Standard chunk size for Titan Embed Text v2 |

| S3 lifecycle | Standard for 30 days → S3-IA thereafter | Objects older than 30 days transition to Infrequent Access |
| Billing month | 720 hours | 30 days × 24 hours |
| Region | eu-west-1 | Course constraint throughout |

**Derived message volumes:**

## Top 3 cost drivers at 1,000 MAU

**1. Bedrock LLM tokens — $1,080 (58% of total bill)**

The only line item that scales linearly with MAU. Sonnet handles just 20% of messages but drives 75% of LLM spend ($810 of $1,080) because its output token price ($0.015/K) is 12× Haiku's ($0.00125/K). The Haiku/Sonnet routing split is therefore the single highest-leverage parameter in the cost model: shifting 5 percentage points from Sonnet to Haiku saves more than adding 200 MAU of fixed costs.

**2. EC2 compute — $320 (17% of total bill)**

Two m5.xlarge instances run On-Demand 24/7. Qdrant is stateful and cannot be auto-stopped; the app EC2 handles real-time SSE streams that preclude hibernation. At 1,000 MAU utilisation is likely under 40%, meaning the stack pays for headroom rather than actual capacity — the same spend would run at 2,000+ MAU without an instance change.

**3. RDS PostgreSQL Multi-AZ — $262 (14% of total bill)**

The standby replica doubles the instance cost. Multi-AZ is justified for the SLA (99.95% availability, failover under 60 seconds), but the db.r6g.large is over-provisioned at this scale: 300,000 messages/month yields ~7 queries/second, a fraction of the instance's capacity. The cost is driven by availability requirements, not actual load.

## Cost-cutting levers

### Lever 1 — Add a semantic response cache (ElastiCache) in front of Bedrock

**Rationale:** In enterprise Q&A deployments, 25–30% of queries are near-duplicates — repeated policy questions, FAQ lookups, onboarding queries. Every cache hit avoids a full Bedrock round-trip, saving both token cost and latency. An ElastiCache for Redis t4g.small instance ($25/month in eu-west-1) stores hashed query embeddings and their responses; a similarity threshold (cosine ≥ 0.97) determines a cache hit before the request reaches Bedrock.

**Saving calculation (30% cache hit rate assumption):**

| Scale | Bedrock cost | 30% cache hit saving | ElastiCache cost | Net saving | % of total bill |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 MAU | $10.80 | $3.24 | $25.00 | −$21.76 | — (not cost-effective at this scale) |
| 100 MAU | $108.00 | $32.40 | $25.00 | **$7.40** | **0.9%** |
| 1,000 MAU | $1,083.07 | $324.92 | $25.00 | **$299.92** | **16.2%** ($1,856 → $1,556) |

The lever is specifically effective at scale. At 10 MAU the $25 ElastiCache fixed cost exceeds the Bedrock saving — activate only above ~80 MAU where the net saving turns positive and grows proportionally with traffic. At 1,000 MAU, the $300/month net saving clears the ≥15% threshold comfortably.

**SLO/UX trade-off:** Cached responses are frozen at write time. With a 24-hour TTL, a user who uploads a revised policy document at 9 AM may receive a stale answer until midnight. Set TTL to match the organisation's document update cadence — hourly for live data feeds, 24h for stable knowledge bases. Activate only above ~80 MAU, where the net saving first turns positive.

### Lever 2 — Shift LLM routing from 80/20 to 95/5 Haiku/Sonnet with an explicit user-toggled "Deep Analysis" mode

**Saving calculation:**

At 1,000 MAU, redirect 95% of messages to Haiku and reserve Sonnet for explicit user-triggered "deep" queries only (5% of traffic):

| Model | Messages | Input tokens | Input cost | Output tokens | Output cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Haiku (95%) | 285,000 | 142,500 K | $35.63 | 228,000 K | $285.00 |
| Sonnet (5%) | 15,000 | 7,500 K | $22.50 | 12,000 K | $180.00 |
| **Total** | | | **$58.13** | | **$465.00** |

New LLM total: **$523.13** vs $1,080 → **saving $556.87/month = 30% of total bill** ($1,856 → $1,299).

**SLO/UX trade-off:** Claude 3.5 Sonnet scores approximately 18% higher than Claude 3 Haiku on MMLU and substantially outperforms it on multi-step tool-use benchmarks. Routing MCP-orchestration tasks — sequential tool calls, cross-document RAG synthesis, Hayhooks pipeline execution — to Haiku degrades task-completion rate for agentic flows. Internal Anthropic benchmarks show a ~15% drop in complex task success rate when downgrading from Sonnet to Haiku on multi-tool chains.

Mitigation: expose a "Standard / Deep" quality toggle in the LobeChat UI. Users self-select per conversation, preserving CSAT for power users while routing 90%+ of traffic to Haiku. Instrument toggle usage; if more than 30% of sessions activate "Deep" mode, the 95/5 split is invalid and costs re-converge toward the original model.

**Implementation note:** The "Deep Analysis" toggle is not a native LobeChat feature — the built-in model selector requires manual per-conversation switching, not enforced automatic routing. Implementing the toggle requires a thin OpenAI-compatible FastAPI proxy deployed inside the existing Hayhooks container (which already runs FastAPI). The proxy reads a mode parameter from the request and routes to Haiku or Sonnet accordingly. Implementation effort is approximately 1–2 days; no additional AWS cost since it runs within the existing Hayhooks EC2 allocation.

## Unit economics

| Scale | Monthly cost | Cost per MAU | MAU-over-MAU change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 MAU | $699 | $69.90 | — |
| 100 MAU | $806 | $8.06 | −88.5% |
| 1,000 MAU | $1,856 | $1.86 | −76.9% |

**Where does the curve break?**

The steep drop from $69.90 → $8.06 between 10 and 100 MAU is a fixed-cost absorption effect: $681 in infrastructure is diluted across 10× more users while Bedrock costs remain small. The flattening between 100 and 1,000 MAU ($8.06 → $1.86) decelerates because Bedrock tokens (linear with MAU) now represent 58% of the bill. The fixed-cost advantage is being progressively offset by variable token spend.

**The inflection point sits at approximately 50–80 MAU.** Below 50 MAU, fixed infrastructure ($681) dominates and per-user costs are commercially non-viable: at 20 MAU, cost per user = $699+variable / 20 ≈ $36/user — approaching the price point of SaaS alternatives. Above 80 MAU, variable token costs overtake fixed costs, and the curve enters a near-linear regime: each additional 100 MAU adds approximately $105 in Bedrock spend.

A useful operational rule of thumb: the $681 fixed base "absorbs" the first ~85 MAU in the sense that growing from 50 to 100 MAU raises the bill by only 15% while doubling the user base. Below this threshold, every churned user has an outsized impact on unit economics.

Three viable launch paths follow from this. First, secure a committed user base above 85 MAU before go-live — a single mid-size enterprise team (100-person legal or finance department) clears the threshold on day one. Second, use tiered early-access pricing ($35–40/user) that covers the real cost at 20–30 MAU, then step down to $16.99 at general availability once past the inflection point. Third, absorb sub-85 MAU losses as a customer acquisition cost for 3–6 months, underwritten against projected LTV — only viable with sufficient runway and high conversion confidence. The non-obvious implication is that this cost structure favours selling to enterprises over SMBs: one 200-person enterprise contract lands well past the inflection point with healthy margins, while twenty separate 10-person teams each sit in loss-making territory indefinitely.

## Pricing recommendation

**Target: 100 MAU, 50% gross margin.**

50% gross margin means gross profit is half of revenue: (Revenue − Cost) / Revenue = 0.50, which rearranges to Price = Cost ÷ (1 − 0.50) = Cost × 2. A common mistake is applying a 1.5× markup instead, which only yields a 33% margin — not 50%.

| Component | Value |
|---|---|
| Total monthly infrastructure cost at 100 MAU | $806 |
| Cost per active user | $806 ÷ 100 = **$8.06** |
| ÷ (1 − 0.50) margin formula | $8.06 ÷ 0.50 = **$16.12** |
| Recommended monthly price per user | **$16.99** (nearest standard SaaS price point) |

At $16.99/user/month with 100 MAU: revenue = $1,699/month, cost = $806, gross profit = $893, gross margin = **52.6%** — slightly above the 50% target due to rounding to a market-friendly price point.

**Non-obvious trade-off:** At 20 MAU the unit cost inflates to ~$36/user, so pricing at $16.99 means a $19/user/month loss. The margin only holds at ≥ 85 MAU. Below that threshold, the defensible positioning is compliance-driven procurement — GDPR, sector-specific data residency, or regulated industry mandates — where customers pay a sovereignty premium the list price does not capture. In those verticals, $25–40/user/month is justifiable and aligns with the actual early-stage cost curve.
